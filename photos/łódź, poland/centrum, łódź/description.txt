Centrum is an area of Łódź designated in 2005 for the Municipal Information System (SIM), not a formal administrative estate but a wayfinding zone for signage and addressing. It lies within the Śródmieście district and covers most of the Katedralna administrative estate. Its boundaries are defined by the edges of Śródmieście where it meets Polesie, Bałuty, Widzew, and Górna, with Kilińskiego Street forming the eastern limit.

The built fabric is overwhelmingly historic. Almost all buildings in the Centrum unit were constructed before 1945, and two-thirds of its dwellings date from before 1918. Łódź escaped heavy wartime destruction, so the centre retains a dense mix of 19th-century tenements, factory complexes, palaces, and interwar public buildings. Post-war planning largely left the historic centre alone, focusing instead on new estates around it, and the area was only maintained or occasionally modernised.

The major post-war intervention in Centrum was the Śródmiejska Dzielnica Mieszkaniowa, known as Łódź Manhattan. Construction began in 1975 and finished in 1982, replacing a row of deteriorating early 19th-century weaver houses on Piotrkowska Street. The complex comprises eight buildings, two of which reach 78 metres and remain the tallest residential towers in the city. Nearly 2,000 flats were delivered, considered comfortable and prestigious at the time, and the towers became a landmark of PRL-era housing.

Revitalisation has since become the dominant force shaping the area. The city has run large-scale programmes targeting historic tenements, courtyards, and public spaces across the centre, with hundreds of millions of złoty invested in renovation and modernisation of the 19th-century building stock. The Nowe Centrum Łodzi programme covers roughly 100 hectares bounded by Narutowicza, Kopcińskiego, Tuwima, and Piotrkowska streets, divided into three zones: a priority area for mixed cultural, commercial, and residential functions; a zone for commercial projects integrated with revitalisation; and a zone of historic late-19th and early-20th-century urban blocks requiring intensive renewal.
