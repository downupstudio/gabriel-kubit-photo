Łódź is the third-largest city in Poland and the capital of Łódź Voivodeship, located in the central part of the country approximately 135 kilometres south-west of Warsaw. The city covers 293.25 square kilometres and sits on the watershed between the Vistula and Oder river basins. It is the largest city in central Poland and its main administrative centre.

The city first appears in written records in 1332, when the village of Łodzia was granted to the bishops of Włocławek. In 1423 King Władysław Jagiełło granted Łódź city rights, and for the next five centuries it functioned as a small agricultural and craft settlement with local significance. As late as 1810 the town had only 190 inhabitants.

The turning point came in 1820, when Stanisław Staszic helped transform the small town into a modern industrial centre. The 1816 decree by the Czar granted territory deeds to German immigrants who came to clear land and build factories and housing. Settlers arrived from southern Germany, Silesia, and Bohemia, as well as from as far as Portugal, England, France, and Ireland. The first cotton mill opened in 1825, and fourteen years later the first steam-powered factory in both Poland and Russia began operations.

The city's industrial development was driven by textile magnates such as Karol Scheibler and Ludwik Grohman. Scheibler opened a cotton spinning mill in 1855 with 5,740 spindles and a 40-horsepower steam engine. In 1921 the Scheibler and Grohman factories merged to create the largest textile plant on the European continent. The city's 19th-century architecture reflects this industrial heritage, with a characteristic blend of residential and industrial functions in the city centre and the distinctive tenement houses with dark courtyard wells.

Łódź is administratively divided into five districts: Śródmieście (the city centre, 713 hectares), Bałuty (7,851 hectares) in the north, Widzew (9,044 hectares) in the east, Górna (7,180 hectares) in the south, and Polesie (4,537 hectares) in the west. Population figures from 2022 show Bałuty as the most populous with 184,605 residents, followed by Górna with 154,064, Widzew with 132,456, Polesie with 130,102, and Śródmieście with 57,217.

In 2005 the city introduced the Municipal Information System (SIM), dividing Łódź into 56 areas for consistent street signage and wayfinding. These SIM areas are not formal administrative units but geographic and historical zones used for orientation purposes.

Łódź has experienced significant demographic decline. The population fell from over 785,000 in 2002 to approximately 655,000 in 2023, a loss of nearly 130,000 residents. This makes Łódź the fastest-depopulating city in Poland. However, an experimental GUS study in 2026 found that 698,600 people actually reside in the city, placing it fourth in Poland after Warsaw, Kraków, and Wrocław.

The city has undertaken extensive revitalisation efforts. The Nowe Centrum Łodzi programme covers a degraded area around the old Łódź Fabryczna station and EC1, with plans for over 3,500 new dwellings and 90,000 square metres of office space. The broader revitalisation programme has restored approximately 180 tenement houses, created 1,006 new dwellings, 270 service premises, and 36 art studios, along with 50 rebuilt or newly constructed street sections. Plac Wolności won first place in a competition for the best-revitalised public space in 2024. In 2024 approximately 42 million złoty was spent on revitalisation-related tasks and projects in the city centre.
